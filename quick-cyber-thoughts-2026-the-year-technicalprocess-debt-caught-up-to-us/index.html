Quick Cyber Thoughts: 2026 - The Year Technical/Process Debt Caught Up to Us

Xavier Santana
November 20, 2026
Updated on November 24, 2026

2026 is drawing to a close, and it's been a big year for cybersecurity and technology in general. We have AI solving some of mathematics' most challenging problems, decompiling and recompiling video games, escaping sandboxes, being used by hackers, and everything in between.

For a lot of people, this is very concerning. But for some, myself included, this is very much a "What did you expect would happen?" moment.

If you're one of those concerned people, you're probably looking at me like I'm crazy, so let me explain myself.

Presuppositions as Root Cause

The Merriam-Webster Dictionary defines presuppositions as follows:

: an act of presupposing or an assumption made in advance : a preliminary supposition : postulate

A lot of the panic, in my opinion, is based on a simple, enticing assumption made in advance: everything we made, from processes to software, was good.

Within this mental model, a lot of AI related things are bad:

  • AI escaping sandboxes and hacking various websites/organizations.
  • AI being able to decompile and recompile video games, including the latest and greatest titles.
  • AI enabling people to jailbreak video game consoles and load unauthorized software onto them.
  • AI helping threat actors find and exploit vulnerabilities before they can be patched.
  • AI coding not being the greatest quality.

There's probably a whole lot more, but you get the idea.

The overall impression is that AI is tearing apart fundamental safeguards and building blocks of society, and helping build less secure software for our future. Humans got it right and now the machines that can work faster and without morality are on the verge of tearing it all down.

It's Minimum Viable Product All the Way Down

One of the greatest things my experience as a video game modder has done is exposed me to the fact that so much of the world basically is running on Minimum Viable Products.

Here's an example:

When XCOM 2's War of the Chosen (WOTC) expansion released, I had to update a mod I had made for the base version. This mod used the game's Suppression function, which allowed player characters to shoot at, but not hit, enemies to lower their aim. However, there was a bug in the WOTC code - Suppression would sometimes use the wrong gun, like a sniper rifle.

Since this was pre-ChatGPT, I dug around in the source code for both versions of the game (available through the mod Software Development Kits) and bashed together a fix for WOTC, which I then published as a mod and submitted to a Github project for game source code modifications. That code was barebones, but effective, and thanks to a fellow contributor, I improved that code and updated the mod.

The game shipped with the bug because the code worked as intended in the limited scenarios it was tested in. I shipped my mod code because it worked in the limited scenarios it was tested in.

The point, you ask? In the past, our ability to test and refine code was basically limited by human skill and time budget. As a result, we built a Jenga tower on top of roughly hewn blocks that might've been riddled with unseen flaws inside.

Our Processes: Minimum Viable Products/Actual Problems

What about our processes, you ask?

Arguably, our processes are just as bad, if not worse, because they sometimes combine two horrible traits:

  • Being minimum viable products that all stakeholders agreed to.
  • Making things as hard as possible on purpose.

The video above is a great example of the latter. The Digital Millennium Copyright Act is a law that makes it borderline impossible to do the following legally and easily:

  • Make video reviews of certain content.
  • Reverse engineer and modify software that is no longer available via first party sales.
  • Reverse engineer software and interfaces used by automobiles to communicate with diagnostic tools.
  • Modify computing hardware you own.
  • Create certain classes of non-profit fan expression without fear of losing your account on certain services.

In addition, the entire DMCA process is designed such that the individual has to prove that they're engaging in fair use, as opposed to the corporation(s) proving that something is a case of unfair use. This leads to ridiculous situations where bots will copyright claim media created and uploaded by a person purportedly on behalf of that person, as well as entire channels/accounts being deleted due to mass copyright strikes for fair use content triggering automatic bans.

For an example of a minimum viable product process, let's look at getting laws passed in the United States, as told by School House Rock:

You might be wondering why I'm using this as an example of a minimum viable product. Ask yourself this question: How does a bad law get taken off the books?

There's two ways that I know of:

  1. Make a new law to repeal the old one.
  2. Have the law ruled unconstitutional generally/as applied to a specific scenario.

The flaw here is that there's no built in error mitigation/obsolescence deprecation process in lawmaking. The simplest and easiest method of doing this is a sunset clause, where a law automatically expires after a set amount of time unless it is renewed. There was actually a proposed rule to implement this, but it hasn't passed.

Why All This Noise Being Made Now?

Now we loop back to the presupposition that everything we made, from processes to software, was good, therefore a lot of AI related events are bad.

On the other hand, if you do not presuppose that anything that has been created is perfect, very little that AI has done is surprising in concept. It might be in execution, in the processes and tools the AI use to accomplish the goals. But we are talking about a technology that can take in large quantities of data, discern patterns, and generate new data based on the inputs. AI being able to find flaws, duplicate things, and misinterpret misconfigured commands is inevitable.

Kathryn Schulz's book, Being Wrong: Adventures in the Margin of Error, touches on a lot of the psychology of why we humans don't like being wrong and have a hard time accepting that its a natural part of our existence. But at the root of all this anxiety about AI doing bad things is something stupid simple:

Humans don't like to feel bad.

AI does something scary? AI does something someone doesn't like? AI reveals that [insert thing here] They feel bad, and because they don't like feeling bad, seek a target to blame as the root cause. Sometimes its the tool, sometimes its the person/people using the tool. There are times when it is correct to blame the tool users, such as when people use AI for cybercrime.

The thing to note about actual bad actors and people who do legally gray things like decompile/recompile games is that they don't feel bad about what they're doing. This gives them an incredible advantage, because they can kick away at the misshapen Jenga towers of code and processes without a care in the world, while the defenders will always be at a disadvantage.

So What Should White Hats Do?

Pivoting bosses and big decision makers from feeling bad about old, possibly outdated paradigms being proven obsolete by AI, to feeling bad about not taking advantage of the technology to completely revamp and improve things from the ground up... and feeling good when they invest in cybersecurity as part of that revamping process.

Some practical targets I can see becoming more viable as the AI bubble seems to be deflating are:

  • Standing up small local AI server deployments to serve as initial layer of offensive/defensive capability.
    • It may be worthwhile to have an AI server dedicated to vulnerability/penetration testing software, while having another handle coding/patching.
  • Using AI to evaluate processes and procedures, then streamlining them to make intended operations as quick and easy as possible.
  • Using AI to decompile and recompile legacy apps into new, modern OS compatible versions/replacements.
    • While Rust is going to be an obvious language to use, the apparent ideological homogeneity of its maintainers makes supply chain poisoning a concerning possibility.
  • Using AI to create tools to interface modern apps with legacy systems/data.

As for consumers/hobbyists/cybersecurity students, it's hard to see what the landscape will be for local AI, because they'll be constrained by three specific hardware costs:

  1. RAM (more is better).
  2. Storage (more is better).
  3. AI processing power/speed.

Next generation CPUs/GPUs/APUs are expected to release next year, but without RAM and storage, improvements to AI processing power are rendered meaningless. Hopefully prices continue to decline and supply increases throughout 2027.

TL;DR

Make peace with the fact that our existing code & processes were never as good as they could be. Don't waste time, energy, and resources on saving old and busted stuff.

Figure out how you/your organization can use AI to improve your code/processes to eliminate as many vulnerabilities as possible.
